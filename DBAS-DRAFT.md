## Standfirst

A permanent museum installation where two strangers pick up old rotary phones and talk to
each other, live, across two museums in Gothenburg.

## 1 · The idea

Pick up the phone in one museum and you are talking, right now, with a stranger standing in
the other one. No screens, no profiles, no matching. Just a voice, and the small act of
dialling and waiting to see whether anyone answers.

That was not the first version. I started with public phone booths between cities, because I
grew up in a small village in Greece where the booth was how I called home, and I still have
the prepaid cards I saved. I made leaflets, I wrote to telecom companies across Sweden and
Denmark, and I went to Ericsson first because the phones I wanted were theirs. Everyone said
no, and the reasons were reasonable. Infrastructure, cost, and nobody wanting to be
responsible for a public phone when something goes wrong in it.

Then I spent time inside museums in Gothenburg and found the Ericofon already sitting in a
case at Röhsska. The exact phone I had been planning to put in a booth, behind glass, where
nobody could touch it. I kept the concept and changed the setting.

> A no is often about the context, not about the idea.

## 2 · The question

We message people on the other side of the world without thinking about it, and somewhere in
there the act of reaching out lost its weight. Museums have the opposite problem. They spend
a lot of effort telling you to look and not touch.

I did not want to just assert that, so before designing anything I went around Gothenburg and
photographed it. Motion alarms at the Maritime Museum that sound if you lean too close to a
garment. Do-not-touch icons in four different visual styles across four institutions. A
queue-ticket dispenser at Röhsska, the kind of thing you have touched a thousand times in a
Swedish shop, sitting under a sign asking you not to.

The question I ended up with:

> What should be considered when designing an interactive museum exhibition exploring themes
> of meaningful telecommunication technologies?

## 3 · Two museums, two installations

One concept, two museums, and two installations that do not look much alike.

Röhsska is a design museum. The piece went into the Innovation room of Design Stories, a few
metres from a case holding Ericofons in every colour. The phone visitors could pick up was
the same model as the ones they could not, which gave the guides something to point at. I had
proposed the Belonging room in my placement document, and the curators moved it. They were
right, and where it landed was never mine to decide alone. The feedback surface there was a
wall of post-its, because the room was already conceptual and already a bit loud.

Mölndals stadsmuseum is a city museum about everyday life, and its Open Storage exhibition
already lets people handle more than 10,000 objects with no glass at all. Putting the Kobra
in there would have been wrong, because the Kobra is a design object and that is a room about
ordinary things. I used the Ericsson Dialog instead, the plain desk phone that sat in normal
Swedish homes, on a wooden table next to a red armchair, with the electronics hidden inside a
vintage box from the museum's own collection. Feedback there was a guestbook rather than
post-its, because a wall of yellow notes would have broken the quiet of a room that is
pretending to be a living room.

The same questions came out differently in each place, and that was the point. Röhsska's
staff wanted it to read as part of an argument about innovation. Mölndal's wanted it to feel
like it had always been in that corner.

## 4 · How I worked

Research through design, because the question cannot be answered on paper. You build the
thing, put it in front of people, and watch what they do with it.

Lab first, in the IxD studio at Chalmers. Sweden has no working landline infrastructure any
more, so an old rotary phone cannot simply be plugged into a wall. I paired the original dial
mechanism with a Bluetooth adapter and a smartphone with a SIM card in it, so the phone
places an ordinary mobile call while the person holding it is still rotating a dial designed
in the 1950s. Finding the phones took months, across Tradera, Facebook Marketplace and
second-hand shops around Gothenburg. I tested nine of them. Most had something broken, a dead
ringer or a dial that would not return or a speaker that crackled, and I did not know how to
repair any of it when I started.

Field next, on campus. Three rounds in the student café and two different buildings, watching
who picked up and who walked past. Fifteen feedback forms, four interviews, and six earlier
conversations with interaction design students who annotated my instruction sheet by hand.

Showroom last, which was the two museums.

The part that does not fit in a process diagram is that finding the second museum took five
months. I kept a collage of the unanswered emails, LinkedIn messages and tailored proposals I
sent to museums and companies across Sweden and Denmark. What actually worked was Jessica at
Röhsska calling someone she knew at Mölndal.

> I changed the design because the pilots told me to, not because I guessed.

## 5 · What I decided

**Voice only.** No screens, no caller ID, nothing that tells you who is on the other end
before you hear them.

**No AI and no recorded voices.** If nobody picks up, nobody picks up. An answering machine
would have made the silence safe, and the silence is part of it.

**Keep the friction, fix the breakage.** The dial stays slow, because the slowness is the
experience. I could have mapped the other museum to a single digit and I decided not to. But
the sound quality in the pilot was genuinely bad and people told me so, and that got fixed
before the museums.

**Do not promise a call.** This one came from the curators rather than from me. I had been
writing signage that said talk to a stranger, and Röhsska's team pushed back: if the phone
rings out, the visitor has failed at something, and that is not a feeling you want to hand
someone in your museum. The poster became *You might connect. You might not. But the moment
is yours.* Everything after that was designed for the call not happening, which is why there
are prompt cards you can read on your own or with whoever you came in with.

**Feedback as reflection.** My structured form, in Röhsska's words, felt like it was trying
to steer the replies in certain directions. It became post-its.

## 6 · It went live

It opened in May 2025 and it is still running.

Visitors arrived in groups and started by watching. Once one person wrote on a post-it,
others followed. Teenagers and young adults were the most likely to actually pick up and
dial. Older visitors more often recognised the Kobra and stood looking at it. Plenty of calls
went unanswered, especially the ones to Mölndal, and people stayed anyway, dialling,
listening to the tone, turning the Rolodex.

Some of what they left behind:

> "Satisfying to dial. The dial tone is eerie."
>
> "I felt scared to talk on the phone. Maybe it reminded me of talking on the house phone
> when I was a kid."
>
> "Social media is the death of sincerity."
>
> "I don't want my mobile phone, I rarely get any pleasure from it. Give me a horse and a
> Kobra phone and I'll be happy."

And an email from Jessica Andersson Sjögerén at Röhsska, on 3 June 2025, the morning of my
thesis defence:

> "Hi Niko! Everything is working well. Our visitors are interacting and we keep moving notes
> to make space for more. The notes are a nice spread from single words and nostalgic
> memories to responses on your questions and philosophical notes. The front staff are also
> confirming this. They want the installation to stay on all summer. Well done!"

## 7 · What I'd tell you

The thesis explored the question rather than settling it, and what came out was a set of
considerations grouped under four lenses. These are the ones I would hand to someone building
something similar.

**What does the voice carry that a text never could?**
*Voice-based communication and emotional presence.* Take the screens out and let the voice do
the work. Place the phone so a person can speak without performing, semi-private but still
visible, because full exposure makes people self-conscious and full privacy invites misuse.
Write the invitation with whoever handles communication at the institution, and do not promise
a connection you cannot guarantee.

**How does it feel to hold a phone that doesn't fit in your pocket?**
*Tactility, materiality and embodied interaction.* Keep the weight, the resistance of the
dial, the mechanical ring. Do not improve them. Make the supporting material analog as well,
so the printed instructions and the card you turn in your hand belong to the same world as
the phone.

**Would you pick up a ringing phone in a public space?**
*Invitation and participation in public space.* People need permission, and the room and the
signage are how you give it. Seed the participation too: leave a few post-its up before
opening, because visitors write far more readily onto a surface that already has writing on
it.

**What happens when something you know suddenly behaves a little bit strange?**
*Defamiliarisation as reflective strategy.* Put the object inside the exhibition's own story
rather than next to it. Avoid screens and apps completely. Write prompts that open a
conversation rather than test knowledge.

## 8 · What this project says about how I work

It is a museum installation, and the work was product work.

It shipped, and it is still running in two public institutions. It was co-designed with five
named people across two museums, none of whom worked for me: a curator, a graphic designer, a
communicator, a museum educator and a curator of pedagogy, plus the technicians and
front-of-house staff who operate it every day. At Röhsska I worked inside their visual
identity using their graphic designer's templates, and the posters look like Röhsska rather
than like me.

I designed the failure state before the success state. *You might connect. You might not.* is
empty-state copy, and it exists because a curator told me my first version would make visitors
feel they had failed at something.

I ran three rounds of testing with twenty-five people and I can tell you which design change
came from which finding. The instruction leaflet went through hand annotation by students and
three revisions before it worked. The microcopy is bilingual and I wrote it with a
communications team.

And it has to run without me. Two institutions operate it daily, which meant writing setup
instructions, troubleshooting steps and a number to call, for receptionists and guides rather
than for designers. It still broke. A lapsed payment on the phone's cellular subscription cut
the service for nearly two hours during the exhibition, and the staff were the ones standing
in front of visitors while it was down. Something that keeps working in a room you are not
in is a different problem from something that demos well.

> In a shared space you design the situation, not just the object.

## 9 · Reflection and moments

I grew up in a small village in Greece, and telecommunication was not a luxury there, it was
how I kept people close. I still have the prepaid cards I saved from calling home. A friend
introduced me to Baudrillard's *Ecstasy of Communication* while we were on a phone call,
which is the kind of thing that stays with you.

The moments I keep:

- The first time someone picked up during the pilot and we had a real conversation.
- Reading the post-its for the first time.
- Calling from the Dialog in Mölndal and talking to a stranger standing in Röhsska.
- Getting the Ericofon tattooed on my arm.

The first thing I would fix is accessibility. It leans hard on sound and on touch, and that
leaves people out. I wrote that in the thesis and it is still true.

Everyone who used it was told, in writing and in both Swedish and English, that no call is
recorded. The photographs of visitors on this page are published with their permission.
