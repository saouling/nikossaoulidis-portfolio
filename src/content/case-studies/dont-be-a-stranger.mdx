<h2 id="problem">The problem</h2>

Pick up the phone in one museum and you are talking, right now, with a stranger standing in the other one. No screens, no profiles, no matching. Just a voice, and the small act of dialling and waiting to see whether anyone answers.

That was not the first version. I started with public phone booths between cities, because I grew up in a small village in Greece where the booth was how I called home, and I still have the prepaid cards I saved. I wrote to telecom companies across Sweden and Denmark, Ericsson first because the phones I wanted were theirs. Everyone said no, and the reasons were reasonable. Then I found the Ericofon already sitting in a case at Röhsska, the exact phone I had been planning to put in a booth, behind glass, where nobody could touch it. I kept the concept and changed the setting.

We message people on the other side of the world without thinking about it, and somewhere in there the act of reaching out lost its weight. Museums have the opposite problem. They spend a lot of effort telling you to look and not touch. I did not want to just assert that, so before designing anything I went around Gothenburg and photographed it: motion alarms at the Maritime Museum, and do-not-touch icons in four different visual styles across four institutions.

<h3>Lab, field, showroom</h3>

Research through design, because the question cannot be answered on paper. You build the thing, put it in front of people, and watch what they do with it.

Lab first, in the IxD studio at Chalmers. Sweden has no working landline infrastructure any more, so I paired the original dial mechanism with a Bluetooth adapter and a smartphone with a SIM card in it. The phone places an ordinary mobile call while the person holding it is still rotating a dial designed in the 1950s. I tested nine second-hand phones, most had something broken, and I did not know how to repair any of it when I started.

Field next, on campus. Three rounds in the student café and two different buildings, watching who picked up and who walked past: fifteen feedback forms, four interviews, and six earlier conversations with interaction design students who annotated my instruction sheet by hand. The setup changed each round.

Showroom last, which was the two museums. Finding the second museum took five months of unanswered emails, LinkedIn messages and tailored proposals to museums and companies across Sweden and Denmark. What actually worked was Jessica at Röhsska calling someone she knew at Mölndal.

<PullQuote>I changed the design because the pilots told me to, not because I guessed.</PullQuote>

<h3>What I decided</h3>

**Voice only.** No screens, no caller ID, nothing that tells you who is on the other end before you hear them.

**No AI and no recorded voices.** If nobody picks up, nobody picks up. An answering machine would have made the silence safe, and the silence is part of it.

**Keep the friction, fix the breakage.** The dial stays slow, because the slowness is the experience. But the sound quality in the pilot was genuinely bad and people told me so, and that got fixed before the museums.

**Do not promise a call.** This one came from the curators. I had been writing signage that said talk to a stranger, and Röhsska's team pushed back: if the phone rings out, the visitor has failed at something. The poster became *You might connect. You might not. But the moment is yours.* Everything after that was designed for the call not happening, which is why there are prompt cards you can read on your own or with whoever you came in with.

<h2 id="how-it-went">How it went</h2>

<FactGrid data={frontmatter.outcome} />

It opened in May 2025 and it is still running. Visitors arrived in groups and started by watching. Once one person wrote on a post-it, others followed. Teenagers and young adults were the most likely to actually pick up and dial. Plenty of calls went unanswered, especially the ones to Mölndal, and people stayed anyway, dialling, listening to the tone, turning the Rolodex.

It is a museum installation, and the work was product work. It was co-designed with five named people across two museums, none of whom worked for me: a curator, a graphic designer, a communicator, a museum educator and a curator of pedagogy, plus the technicians and front-of-house staff who operate it every day. At Röhsska I worked inside their visual identity using their graphic designer's templates, and the posters look like Röhsska rather than like me.

And it has to run without me. Two institutions operate it daily, so I wrote setup and troubleshooting instructions for receptionists and guides rather than for designers. It still broke. A lapsed payment on the phone's cellular subscription cut the service for nearly two hours during the exhibition, and the staff were the ones standing in front of visitors while it was down. Something that keeps working in a room you are not in is a different problem from something that demos well.

<h2 id="what-i-learned">What I learned</h2>

<FactGrid data={frontmatter.learnings} tone="ink" />

I designed the failure state before the success state. *You might connect. You might not.* is empty-state copy, and it exists because a curator told me my first version would make visitors feel they had failed at something. I ran three rounds of testing with twenty-five people and I can tell you which design change came from which finding. The instruction leaflet went through hand annotation by students and three revisions before it worked.

The thesis ended in four lenses, the considerations I would hand to anyone building something similar.

<details class="lens">
  <summary>What does the voice carry that a text never could?</summary>
  <div class="lens-body">

**Voice-based communication and emotional presence.** Take the screens out and let the voice do the work. Place the phone so a person can speak without performing, semi-private but still visible. Do not promise a connection you cannot guarantee.

  </div>
</details>

<details class="lens">
  <summary>How does it feel to hold a phone that doesn't fit in your pocket?</summary>
  <div class="lens-body">

**Tactility, materiality and embodied interaction.** Keep the weight, the resistance of the dial, the mechanical ring. Do not improve them. Make the supporting material analog as well, so the printed instructions and the card you turn in your hand belong to the same world as the phone.

  </div>
</details>

<details class="lens">
  <summary>Would you pick up a ringing phone in a public space?</summary>
  <div class="lens-body">

**Invitation and participation in public space.** People need permission, and the room and the signage are how you give it. Seed the participation too: leave a few post-its up before opening, because visitors write far more readily onto a surface that already has writing on it.

  </div>
</details>

<details class="lens">
  <summary>What happens when something you know suddenly behaves a little bit strange?</summary>
  <div class="lens-body">

**Defamiliarisation as reflective strategy.** Put the object inside the exhibition's own story rather than next to it. Avoid screens and apps completely. Write prompts that open a conversation rather than test knowledge.

  </div>
</details>

<PullQuote>In a shared space you design the situation, not just the object.</PullQuote>
