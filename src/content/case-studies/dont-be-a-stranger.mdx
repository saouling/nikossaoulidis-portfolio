<h2 id="problem">Pick up <span class="h-sub">The problem</span></h2>

Pick up the phone in one museum and you are talking, right now, with a stranger standing in the other one. No screens, no profiles, no matching. Just a voice, and the small act of dialling and waiting to see whether anyone answers.

I grew up in a small village in Greece where the phone booth was how I called home, and I still have the prepaid cards I saved. The first version of this project was booths between cities. I wrote to telecom companies in Sweden and Denmark, Ericsson first. They said no, or never answered. Then I found the Ericofon, the exact phone I had in mind, already in a case at Röhsska: behind glass, where nobody could touch it.

That was the problem in one picture. We message people on the other side of the world without thinking, and reaching out has lost its weight. Museums have the opposite habit: they spend a lot of effort telling you to look and not touch. I photographed it across Gothenburg before designing anything.

<h2 id="what-i-did">Dial <span class="h-sub">What I did</span></h2>

The question can't be answered on paper, so this was research through design: build the thing, put it in front of people, watch what they do. It took three stages.

<DbasProcess />

Sweden has no working landline network any more, so in the lab I paired each dial with a Bluetooth adapter and a smartphone with a SIM card. You turn a dial designed in the 1950s and it places an ordinary mobile call. I tested nine second-hand phones. Most had something broken, and I did not know how to repair any of it when I started.

Then three rounds on campus, in a café and two corridors, watching who picked up and who walked past. Twenty-five people took part across the pilots.

I coded the interviews and forms with reflexive thematic analysis. Five themes came out of it, and each one changed something before the museums. I can tell you which design change came from which finding.

<ThemesLedger />

Even the instructions were tested. Interaction design students annotated my leaflet by hand, and the version that went into the museums had a step mine had missed.

<h2 id="wait">Wait <span class="h-sub">Designing for nobody answering</span></h2>

*And wait.* The students were right, and it turned out to be the whole project.

I waited on the museums first. Röhsska said yes in November 2024, months before the thesis officially started. Finding the second museum took five months of emails, LinkedIn messages and tailored proposals, and most of them were never answered. My plan B was both phones in two rooms of the same museum. What finally worked was Jessica at Röhsska calling someone she knew at Mölndal.

<GhostWall />

Then the visitors would wait. I had been writing signage that said *talk to a stranger*, and Röhsska's team pushed back: if the phone rings out, the visitor has failed at something. The poster became *You might connect. You might not. But the moment is yours.* From there I designed for the call not happening. Voice only, with no screen and no caller ID. No AI and no recorded voices: an answering machine would have made the silence safe, and the silence is part of it. And there are prompt cards on a Rolodex, so you can read them on your own or with whoever you came in with.

<CallPoster />

And sometimes the system itself would make people wait. A lapsed payment on the phone's cellular subscription cut the service for nearly two hours during the exhibition, and the museum staff were the ones standing in front of visitors while it was down. So I wrote setup and troubleshooting instructions for receptionists and guides, not for designers. Something that keeps working in a room you are not in is a different problem from something that demos well.

<h2 id="how-it-went">Talk <span class="h-sub">How it went</span></h2>

<FactGrid data={frontmatter.outcome} />

One concept, two museums, and two installations that do not look much alike. Getting there was mostly coordination. I was the only designer and researcher, and everyone else the installation needed belonged to someone else: curators, a graphic designer, a communicator, educators, the technicians, guides and receptionists who run it every day. I came to every meeting with something they could react to, and left every meeting with decisions written down and tasks handed to the right person.

<Coordination />

At Röhsska I worked inside the museum's visual identity and its graphic designer's templates, so the posters look like Röhsska rather than like me.

<MuseumCompare />

It opened in May 2025 and it is still running. Visitors arrived in groups and started by watching. Once one person wrote on a post-it, others followed. Teenagers and young adults were the most likely to pick up and dial. An older man tried it alone, then called his partner over. A group of schoolboys tried to decode how the dial worked. Plenty of calls went unanswered, especially the ones to Mölndal, and people stayed anyway, dialling, listening to the tone, turning the Rolodex.

<h2 id="what-i-learned">Hang up <span class="h-sub">What I learned</span></h2>

<FactGrid data={frontmatter.learnings} tone="ink" />

I designed the failure state before the success state. *You might connect. You might not.* is empty-state copy, and it exists because a curator told me my first version would make visitors feel they had failed. In a shared space the room, the permission and the silence do as much work as the object.

<LensGrid />

<PullQuote>In a shared space you design the situation, not just the object.</PullQuote>

The first thing I would fix is accessibility. It leans hard on sound and on touch, and that leaves people out. I wrote that in the thesis and it is still true.
