<h2 id="problem">The problem</h2>

Private 5G software is dense. A lot of state, a lot of configuration, a lot of things that go wrong in ways only an expert would recognise. The interesting part of the job was rarely the screen itself. It was working out what a network engineer actually needs to see at the moment they look.

Most of what I'm proudest of ended up being about the space between design and engineering, rather than the design on its own.

<SubRecord
  id="case-1"
  no="01a"
  title="Making our Figma deliveries AI-readable"
  lead="Our developers used AI to turn Figma into code, but it kept building the annotations instead of the interface, so I built a way to fix that."
  part="Research, an audit framework, and the delivery-for-ai skill"
  result="Adopted as the team-wide standard"
/>

Developers in our organisation were using AI agents to generate UI code straight from the UX team's Figma files. The files were built for humans: screens buried under sticky notes, arrows, colour overlays, design rationale sitting right on top of the interface. The AI could not tell the difference. It tried to build the sticky notes too. As one of our developers put it, "Annotations confuse the AI."

<div class="wide">
  <DeliveryBeforeAfter />
</div>

First I went and looked. I documented two real developer workflows in our organisation to understand where it actually broke, and set up a Slack channel so the feedback could keep coming rather than arriving once at the end. Then I read around: about twenty case studies, industry write-ups from teams like Atlassian, Spotify, Miro and Figma, plus academic papers. The pattern was the same everywhere: structured input produces better output.

I also needed a way to say how bad our files were, so I put together a small audit framework: five dimensions, scored out of ten. It is my own framework rather than an industry standard, but it meant we could talk about a file being more or less AI-ready instead of just "messy".

<AuditFramework />

Then I built a Kiro skill called **delivery-for-ai**. You point it at a polish page and it produces a clean, AI-readable delivery page in the same file: on the left, clean screens organised into feature-specific sections; on the right, a small number of structured notes on interaction behaviour, style tokens, and a summary of what changed.

<PullQuote>The rule that made it work: the screens show what it looks like, the notes say how it behaves. Never both.</PullQuote>

<SpecRule />

When I let the notes describe the interface as well, the AI started inventing things that weren't there. So I kept them strictly about behaviour. I tested it with a developer over four rounds and fixed what broke each time, then wrote the whole approach up as a shared standard so it wasn't just a thing I did on my own files.

PM to design, design to front-end, front-end to back-end. Everyone reacting to what the person before them already decided, and nobody in the room when the decision actually got made. What we ended up asking for is smaller than it sounds: invite one person from another discipline one step earlier than you normally would. You are giving them context, not extra work.

<HandoffLoop />

We did not want to hand out principles. Principles are easy to nod along to and impossible to act on. So every method came with a time box, a group size and a source, because "collaborate more" is not something anyone can put in a calendar.

<HandoutSheet />

Workshops are the easy part. None of it sticks unless the ordinary days change too, which is why half the handout is daily habits rather than methods.

I also helped run an AI design-review forum with Jakob Kitzing and Mats Kullerstrand, which came out of the same instinct: fewer handovers, more looking at things together.

<SubRecord
  id="case-3"
  no="01c"
  title="Generative UI for data-dense dashboards"
  lead="A hackathon project on dashboards that assemble themselves from context, and what building it taught me about where UX is going."
  part="The card component, the specs, the prototype and the proposal, in a Tweak Week team with three other designers and a front-end developer"
  result="A US back-end team used the component to display live data"
/>

Our dashboards hold an enormous amount of telecom data. What any one person needs to see depends on their role and on what the network is doing right now. A static dashboard can't keep up with that, and building a new one for every case is slow.

Before anything clever, the card had to work. It was a fixed-size box that broke the moment a layout became dynamic, so we rebuilt it as a content-driven component that responds to whatever width the grid gives it. One component instead of a set of special cases.

<div class="wide">
  <CardSystem />
</div>

Generative UI is easy to get excited about, so I went looking for what had actually been shown.

<ul style="list-style: disc; padding-left: 1.25em; max-width: var(--measure)">
  <li>Stanford and Georgia Tech, 2025: people prefer dynamic card interfaces over chat, by roughly 72%.</li>
  <li>Google Research, 2025: LLM-generated interfaces match expert designs about half the time, and constrained component catalogs beat free-form generation.</li>
  <li>IEEE Drillboards, 2025: dashboards should adapt to how expert the user is.</li>
</ul>

The proposal was an assistant and a canvas, roughly 30% steering panel, 70% canvas. The assistant assembles a whole layout from context rather than one card at a time. Cards arrive pre-filled and you can edit, pin or dismiss them, and you steer it two ways: talk to it, or just move the cards yourself. The deliverable wasn't a screen. It was a system.

<h2 id="how-it-went">How it went</h2>

<FactGrid data={frontmatter.outcome} />

The delivery skill went from the AI inventing a toolbar that didn't exist to a developer saying "this is pretty good, the issues are on my side now." We handed out a one-page version of the methods at the conference so people could start the next day without needing us in the room. That mattered more to me than the talk did. A talk is forty minutes and then it is over. The handout is still on people's desks. And from the dashboard specs, a front-end developer adapted the real component, and a back-end team in the US used it to display live data and graphs.

<h2 id="what-i-learned">What I learned</h2>

<FactGrid data={frontmatter.learnings} tone="ink" />

Clean, organised input produces better code with fewer mistakes. That sounds obvious written down, but it changes what a design deliverable is for. And the first case only worked because I built it with the developers rather than for them. The second was Frida and me trying to make that normal instead of lucky.

<PullQuote>UX is moving from designing screens to designing systems of rules.</PullQuote>

You end up writing for two audiences at once, humans and AI agents, and they want different things from the same document. Version control and specs are quietly becoming design tools. And the valuable part of the job shifts towards defining the constraints, then evaluating and curating what comes back.
