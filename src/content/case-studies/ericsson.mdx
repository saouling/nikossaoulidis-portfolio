<h2>The context</h2>

Private 5G software is dense. A lot of state, a lot of configuration, a lot of things that go wrong in ways only an expert would recognise. The interesting part of the job was rarely the screen itself. It was working out what a network engineer actually needs to see at the moment they look.

Most of what I'm proudest of ended up being about the space between design and engineering, rather than the design on its own.

<h2 id="case-1">Case 1 · Making our Figma deliveries AI-readable</h2>

One line: our developers used AI to turn Figma into code, but it kept building the annotations instead of the interface, so I built a way to fix that.

<h3>The problem</h3>

Developers in our organisation were using AI agents to generate UI code straight from the UX team's Figma files. The files were built for humans: screens buried under sticky notes, arrows, colour overlays, design rationale sitting right on top of the interface.

The AI could not tell the difference. It tried to build the sticky notes too.

<PullQuote cite="one of our developers">"Annotations confuse the AI."</PullQuote>

<h3>What I did</h3>

First I went and looked. I documented two real developer workflows in our organisation to understand where it actually broke, and set up a Slack channel so the feedback could keep coming rather than arriving once at the end.

Then I read around. About twenty case studies, industry write-ups from teams like Atlassian, Spotify, Miro and Figma, plus academic papers. The pattern was the same everywhere: structured input produces better output. Every team that cleaned up what they gave the AI saw the quality go up.

I also needed a way to say how bad our files were, so I put together a small audit framework: five dimensions, scored out of ten. It is my own framework rather than an industry standard, but it meant we could talk about a file being more or less AI-ready instead of just "messy".

<h3>The tool</h3>

I built a Kiro skill called **delivery-for-ai**. You point it at a polish page and it produces a clean, AI-readable delivery page in the same file.

The structure is simple. On the left, clean screens organised into feature-specific sections. On the right, a small number of structured notes: interaction behaviour, style tokens, and a summary of what changed.

<PullQuote>The rule that made it work: the screens show what it looks like, the notes say how it behaves. Never both.</PullQuote>

When I let the notes describe the interface as well, the AI started inventing things that weren't there. So I kept them strictly about behaviour.

<h3>Testing it</h3>

I tested it with a developer over four rounds and fixed what broke each time.

<PullQuote>It went from the AI inventing a toolbar that didn't exist to "this is pretty good, the issues are on my side now."</PullQuote>

I wrote the whole approach up as a shared standard so it wasn't just a thing I did on my own files.

<h3>What I take from it</h3>

Clean, organised input produces better code with fewer mistakes. That sounds obvious written down, but it changes what a design deliverable is for. And it only worked because I built it with the developers rather than for them.

<h2 id="case-2">Case 2 · Build better together</h2>

One line: design and development kept working in sequence instead of together, so Frida Edstam and I put together a set of methods people could start using the next morning, and gave a talk about it at the Ericsson Developer Conference.

<h3>The handoff trap</h3>

PM to design, design to front-end, front-end to back-end. Everyone reacting to what the person before them already decided, and nobody in the room when the decision actually got made.

What we ended up asking for is smaller than it sounds: invite one person from another discipline one step earlier than you normally would.

<PullQuote>You are giving them context, not extra work.</PullQuote>

<h3>Three ways to actually work together</h3>

We did not want to hand out principles. Principles are easy to nod along to and impossible to act on. So every method came with a time box, a group size and a source, because "collaborate more" is not something anyone can put in a calendar.

<div class="col-grid cols-3">
  <div>
    <h3>Brainwriting</h3>
    <p>15 to 20 minutes, 3 to 8 people, mixed. Write the problem up where everyone can see it. Everyone writes ideas in silence for five minutes, rotates the papers and builds on each other's for three, then discusses the strongest directions. Nobody dominates, and you get more ideas in less time.</p>
  </div>
  <div>
    <h3>UX and front-end pairing</h3>
    <p>15 to 30 minutes, one designer and one developer. Sit at the same screen when you review a feature. Small issues get fixed on the spot, bigger ones get ticketed together. The designer sees the real constraints, the developer gets clarity on the intent.</p>
  </div>
  <div>
    <h3>UX bug bash</h3>
    <p>30 to 60 minutes, 2 to 5 people, mixed. Developers and designers sitting together trying to break the experience rather than the code: behaviour, copy, empty and error states, edge cases like long text or a slow connection.</p>
  </div>
</div>

<h3>Four habits for the rest of the week</h3>

Workshops are the easy part. None of it sticks unless the ordinary days change too: public channels instead of DMs, mini demos the moment something is visual, a habit of inviting one person from another discipline into your next session, and a dedicated UX improvements channel anyone can report into.

<h3>We printed it</h3>

We handed out a one-page version at the conference so people could start the next day without needing us in the room. That mattered more to me than the talk did. A talk is forty minutes and then it is over. The handout is still on people's desks.

<h2 id="case-3">Case 3 · Generative UI for data-dense dashboards</h2>

One line: a hackathon project on dashboards that assemble themselves from context, and what building it taught me about where UX is going.

<p class="fs-small"><strong>Credit where it's due:</strong> this was a Tweak Week team project with three other designers and a front-end developer. My part was the card component, the specs, the prototype and the proposal.</p>

<h3>The problem</h3>

Our dashboards hold an enormous amount of telecom data. What any one person needs to see depends on their role and on what the network is doing right now. A static dashboard can't keep up with that, and building a new one for every case is slow.

<h3>Fix the foundation first</h3>

Before anything clever, the card had to work. It was a fixed-size box that broke the moment a layout became dynamic, and it limited what data we could show at all.

So we rebuilt it as a content-driven component that responds to whatever width the grid gives it. One component instead of a set of special cases.

<h3>Grounded in research, not vibes</h3>

Generative UI is easy to get excited about, so I went looking for what had actually been shown.

<ul style="list-style: disc; padding-left: 1.25em; max-width: var(--measure)">
  <li>Stanford and Georgia Tech, 2025: people prefer dynamic card interfaces over chat, by roughly 72%.</li>
  <li>Google Research, 2025: LLM-generated interfaces match expert designs about half the time, and constrained component catalogs beat free-form generation.</li>
  <li>IEEE Drillboards, 2025: dashboards should adapt to how expert the user is.</li>
</ul>

Across the industry guidance the same principles kept coming back: design for outcomes rather than screens, keep containers consistent, let AI suggest and humans approve, and always show where the data came from and how fresh it is.

<h3>The proposal</h3>

An assistant and a canvas. Roughly 30% steering panel, 70% canvas.

The assistant assembles a whole layout from context rather than one card at a time. Cards arrive pre-filled and you can edit, pin or dismiss them. You steer it two ways: talk to it, or just move the cards yourself.

<PullQuote>The deliverable wasn't a screen. It was a system.</PullQuote>

<h3>Building it</h3>

We built a flexible card component in Figma, wrote the component specs in markdown and versioned them in GitLab, then generated a working HTML prototype straight from those specs to check they held up. A front-end developer adapted the real component from the same specs, and a back-end team in the US used it to display live data and graphs. Then we shared what we found with other teams.

<h3>What it taught me</h3>

This is the part I keep thinking about.

<PullQuote>UX is moving from designing screens to designing systems of rules.</PullQuote>

You end up writing for two audiences at once, humans and AI agents, and they want different things from the same document. Version control and specs are quietly becoming design tools. And the valuable part of the job shifts towards defining the constraints, then evaluating and curating what comes back.
