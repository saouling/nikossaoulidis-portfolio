The EWP Dashboard is the tool European universities use to manage Erasmus+ student mobility. When I joined Aristotle University's IT Center, there wasn't a UX role on the team at all. Nobody was looking at why the tool was hard to use, so I made that my job.

<h2>The problem</h2>

It had grown into a hub of modules built at different times, Accounts, Learning Agreements, Institutional Agreements, Erasmus+ applications, so it felt like several separate apps stitched together rather than one tool. The interface complaints I kept hearing were only the symptoms. Underneath them: inconsistent components across modules, no shared visual language, and every team that had built a different part solving the same problem its own way. So instead of patching screens one at a time, I treated the whole thing as one system.

<h2>What I did</h2>

<h3>Research</h3>

I designed and ran a survey, 17 questions, close to 1,000 institutions responding. The complaints were specific: too much unnecessary information always present, no way to print a PDF version of an agreement, listings that were unintuitive and confusing. Two patterns stood out beyond the individual complaints: everything was shown at once regardless of relevance, and most people needed to do at least some of this from their phone.

I mapped status to colour throughout the interface, accept, reject, pending, so people could scan a long list of applications at a glance instead of reading every row, leaning on Jacob's Law and recognition over recall rather than my own instinct for what would help. I presented the findings to over 1,000 users and stakeholders at a European University Foundation webinar, then ran a later task-based usability survey to measure whether it actually got easier, not just whether people said it did.

<h3>Design system &amp; visual identity</h3>

I built a design system, based on Bootstrap, as the foundation for the new interface: a type scale, a button system with every interaction state (active, hover, click, disabled), and one consistent icon set, so the same component behaved the same way in every module instead of five different ways. I also designed the visual identity, including a new logo, replacing a placeholder mark with a proper brand.

<h3>Prototyping &amp; development</h3>

I prototyped the redesign in Figma, handed it to the development team, then stayed close through the build so the shipped product actually matched the spec and stayed as usable as the prototype promised.

<h3>Collaboration &amp; leadership</h3>

I led four developers, keeping constant communication between them and the European University Foundation's stakeholders so the technical build tracked the design intent. It was the first time I'd managed a build like this, and it pushed my communication and project-management skills harder than the design work itself did.
