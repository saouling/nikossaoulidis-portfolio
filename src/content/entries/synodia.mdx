Streaming made music endless and frictionless, and easy to skip past without really listening. Synodia goes the other way. It plays one song for about a day, and that song comes from a friend, not an algorithm. The name is Greek, from *syn* (together) and *odos* (the way), a music journey in company.

It works in three moves. You sync your devices with a few friends by cabling them into a loop. During the day, when a song catches you, you press record. At the end of the listening interval everyone receives a song from someone in the loop, with a small printed receipt instead of a screen: the cover, the song, the artist and the day it arrived.

The design leans on Dieter Rams's work for Braun and on Teenage Engineering's physical controls: a disc to turn, a spark wheel, and a mirrored version for the roughly one in ten people who are left-handed. I sketched it, made rough physical forms to feel how it sits in the palm, and wrote it a love letter, a method for finding what you actually care about in an object.

I tested the idea without the object: three friends in a group chat, playing the loop by hand over three listening intervals. All three sent songs in the first two, two of three in the third. They called it fun but a little underwhelming as a chat, since sharing songs is something they already do, and all agreed it would hold their interest as a physical thing. The surprise was the timing: requests arriving after midnight or early in the morning, off the usual daily rhythm, which turned out to be part of the charm.
