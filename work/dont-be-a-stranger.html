Don't Be a Stranger

A permanent museum installation where two strangers pick up old rotary phones and talk to each other, live, across two museums in Gothenburg.

Röhsska Museum, filmed on site.

Role
Sole designer and researcher. Research, build, install, all of it.
When
2025. MSc thesis, Chalmers University of Technology.
Where
Röhsska Museum and Mölndals City Museum.
Type
Research through design. Physical installation.

Running permanently in both museums for a year and three months. People stay on the phone with a stranger for five to twenty minutes.

The idea

Pick up the phone in one museum and you are suddenly talking, right now, with a stranger standing in the other one. No screens, no profiles, no matching. Just a voice, and the small act of dialling and waiting for someone to answer.

The phone is an Ericofon, the one people in Sweden call the Kobra.

One number in each museum. Pick up, dial, and you're talking to a stranger standing in the other one, right now.

My first idea was connected phone booths between cities. I made leaflets, I pitched telecom companies, I started with Ericsson. Nobody backed it.

Then I spent time inside the museums and realised a museum was the right home for it all along. I kept the concept and changed the setting.

A “no” is often about the context, not about the idea.

The question

We message people on the other side of the world without thinking about it, and somewhere in there the act of reaching out lost its weight. Museums have the opposite problem: they spend a lot of effort telling you to look and not touch.

The research question

What should be considered when designing an interactive museum exhibition about meaningful communication?

Röhsska Museum

Roughly 100,000 visitors a year. Its Design Stories exhibition groups the collection into six themes — desire, innovation, creativity, efficiency, change, belonging — and adds hands-on stations like a chair you build yourself. Even so, most of it stays look-only.

Mölndals stadsmuseum

Its Open Storage exhibition holds over 10,000 everyday objects with no glass cases at all. Visitors are already expected to touch, just not to talk to a stranger through what they're touching.

Phone booths between cities, first — connecting two distant locations by turning a dial. Theory pulled from three fields: communication, museum studies, interaction design. Then documenting how museums actually handle touch, before building anything.

The pivot to museums. Leaflets and pitches to find a partner, starting with Ericsson. Sourcing the actual phones, and the Bluetooth hardware needed to make an old rotary handset carry a live call over a normal phone network.

The first working prototype, on a desk in the IxD studio, tested with six IxD students. Two phones, a power strip, a laptop, and a lot of tangled cable.

Full install on campus. Watched who picked up and who walked past, then reworked the supporting material and the phones themselves based on what actually happened, not what I assumed would happen.

Rebuilt from what v.1 showed. Fifteen feedback forms and four interviews this time, collected formally instead of just observed.

Co-designed with curators, communicators and graphic designers, across two institutions. Live, permanently, since May 2025.

I ran it as research through design, because the question can't be answered on paper. You build something real and watch what happens. Lab, then field, then the real thing, each step adding realism and stakes. The output was five transferable design guidelines, not just the installation.

I changed the design because the pilots told me to, not because I guessed.

The don't-touch instinct

Museums train people to observe, not act. How do you get a stranger to pick up and talk?

A safe, meaningful call

An anonymous live call with a stranger can be intimidating, awkward, or just fall flat. How do you make it feel worth it?

Many voices, two museums

Curators, pedagogues, communicators, graphic designers, two institutions, all with different priorities. How do you get everyone aligned?

What I decided

Give permission

Warm, co-designed signage and instructions, Rolodex prompt cards so people know what to say, placement that's visible but a little sheltered.

Swedish and English, same size, same weight.

Fine-tune friction

Keep the weight, the dial, the wait, on purpose, because the ritual is what makes it meaningful. Then scaffold it with voice-only anonymity, prompts and clear instructions, all tuned by testing.

I kept the friction on purpose. The discomfort is what makes it feel like it matters.

Lead and align

Tailored pitches and co-design sessions so each stakeholder shaped their own piece rather than reviewing mine. Two decisions that came directly out of those sessions, kept in the words they were made in:

Once it was live, children started making prank calls and the noise began disturbing other visitors. I solved it with the curators, through where the piece sits in the room and by covering it during children's visits.

In a shared space you design the situation, not just the object.

Five guidelines

The installation was the artefact. The actual output of the thesis was a set of design guidelines for anyone building an interactive museum exhibition about communication, drawn from five themes that ran across every phase of testing, pre-pilot interviews, the campus pilot, and the museum feedback.

01

Emotional presence through analog voice

Kept it voice-only and anonymous. No screens, no names, no visual feedback, even where I could have added them.

02

Friction and vulnerability as invitations

Separated meaningful friction from broken friction. Fixed the sound quality and stability; kept the weight, the dial, the wait.

03

Defamiliarisation and improvisation

Explained enough, left room for exploration. The prompt cards help a conversation without prescribing it.

04

Social context and spatial norms

Placement became a real design decision. Semi-sheltered but visible, with signage that gives permission.

05

Material memory and nostalgia

Kept the original hardware rather than replicating it. The weight, the click of the dial, is the entry point.

What I learned

  • Design for the situation, not just the object.
  • Research and testing beat instinct.
  • What delights me can intimidate someone else, so I design for their comfort, not mine.
  • Plans slip. Stay calm when they do.
  • Aligning people is the hard part, and it's a skill you build, not an afterthought.

Reflection

I grew up in a small village in Greece, and telecommunication wasn't a luxury for me, it was how I kept people close. I saved every prepaid phone card from calling home, and I still remember playing with my great-grandmother's rotary phone as a kid, just to hear the dial click back into place.

A friend introduced me to Baudrillard's Ecstasy of Communication — while we were on a phone call. That's when I started noticing how much modern media floods us with signals while emptying out the depth of actually talking to someone.

McLuhan said the medium is the message.
